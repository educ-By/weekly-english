Virtually all cultures and epochs fizz with magic. You might call it a secret ingredient of human life, because magic takes so many forms and serves so many functions.

One relatively new area of research among historians and archaeologists focuses on the use of magic and mysticism to protect buildings, gardens and their inhabitants. It’s one of the many forms of magic I explore in my forthcoming book, Magick: Decoding the Visual Culture of Spells, Illusions and Psychic Powers .

Early modern Europe saw numerous reports of towns being burned by “ incendiary witchcraft ” or fire-starting demons. One such act of arson reportedly took place in Schiltach , Germany, in 1533. These dramatic examples, from an era of terrifyingly combustible domiciles, help explain why so many people focused magical building protection on chimneys and around hearths.

Magick tells the visual story of the magical arts, from personal and community-based folk magic to ceremonial magic, illusionists and the paranormal.

Even today, people living comparatively safe and comfortable lives still use special objects and occult techniques to allay their anxieties and increase their sense of security.

A few years ago, a lovely volunteer guiding me around an ancient church in North Yorkshire, England, explained that she had planted a rowan tree in her garden. The purpose? To repel her neighbor’s “evil eye.” Rowan trees have been used in this way for centuries across the British Isles.

So, if you’ve got problem neighbors, or if your house and garden seem to suffer from bad luck, perhaps it’s time to try a magical solution.

Trees, herbs and other plants were and are used to defend properties against dangers both natural and supernatural. The Complete Herbal , a book by 17th-century botanist Nicholas Culpeper , claimed that a bay tree in the garden would protect a home from witchcraft, the devil and lightning.

Betony , meanwhile, was said to be helpful against diseases, epidemics and witchcraft, while bittersweet nightshade was believed to be effective against harmful sorcery.

Across Britain and Europe, a rosemary bush in the garden was supposed to protect from witches who attacked at night. They would be caught up counting every leaf until the light of morning dispelled their evil powers. In the Americas, chile peppers have long been regarded as evil-averting and were sometimes fixed to doors to repel harmful powers.

In early modern Europe and beyond, five- and six-pointed stars were inscribed into wood and mortar to repel evil.

Magical instructional texts known today as “ grimoires ”—including the tiny Booke of Experiments (1622) now held in the University of Oxford’s Bodleian Library—explain that these star symbols helped overcome enemies.
